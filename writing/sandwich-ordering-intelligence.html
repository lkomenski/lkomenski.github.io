From 191-column CSV to an ordering decision: scoping a data tool for a problem POS systems don't solve

Business Analysis · Technical Scoping · Contractor Proposal · 10 min read

From the role

Sandwich Ordering Intelligence Tool

Independent contractor project · Local food & beverage business · Tacoma, WA

Proposal delivered · Awaiting decision
Status note

This tool has not been built yet. The proposal has been delivered and a decision is pending. This post is about the analysis, the scoping, and the proposal — because that work is the portfolio artifact at this stage. The thinking is the work.

The business had been ordering sandwiches the same way for years: one person, institutional memory, gut feel about what sold last week. The POS system captured every transaction automatically — item-level sales data, updated daily. But surfacing that data for an ordering decision meant either reading an aggregate dashboard that showed totals without patterns, or exporting a 191-column CSV that nobody had time to parse during an order shift.

The data existed. The gap was between the export and the decision. And the person who had historically bridged that gap with experience and memory was gone.

I identified the problem independently, analyzed the existing sales data to understand what was actually there, scoped a phased technical solution, researched the competitive landscape, and delivered a formal contractor proposal — all before any build began. Here's what that process looked like.

What the data analysis showed

The first step was understanding whether the POS data was actually useful for ordering decisions — and if so, what it could and couldn't tell you. I pulled approximately 1,278 records of item-level sales data covering March through May 2026, across roughly 30 sandwich items.

Finding 01

Day-of-week patterns were clear and consistent

Aggregating sales by item and day of week revealed patterns that were strong enough to drive ordering decisions. Some items sold reliably on specific days. Others had essentially flat demand across the week. The patterns were there — they just weren't visible in the existing dashboard view, which collapsed everything into weekly or monthly totals.

Finding 02

External factors are structurally invisible to POS systems

Sales volume on any given day is shaped by things that never touch the POS: weather, school calendars, local events, seasonal patterns. A hot Tuesday in June produces different sandwich demand than a rainy Tuesday in November — but the POS records both as "Tuesday" with no indication of why they differed. Any ordering tool that relies purely on historical averages will systematically under- or over-order on days where external conditions push demand away from the norm. Those factors have to be engineered in from outside the POS.

Finding 03

The ordering problem has the same root cause as the staffing problem

This is the same structural issue I identified when rebuilding the staffing framework: institutional knowledge living in a person, not in a system, with no documented baseline to fall back on when that person leaves. Ordering decisions and staffing decisions are both judgment calls that could be grounded in data but historically weren't. The POS has been capturing the relevant data for both problems the entire time. The gap isn't the data — it's the absence of a tool that makes that data usable at the moment a decision needs to be made.

Why existing tools don't solve this

Before scoping a custom build, I looked at what already existed: the POS vendor's native data connector, third-party forecasting tools, and inventory management platforms in the same category.

The POS vendor's data connector surfaces the same aggregate dashboards and CSV exports the business was already ignoring — it doesn't add ordering intelligence, just data access. Forecasting tools in this space are priced and designed for mid-size restaurant chains with dedicated ops staff; the pricing structure alone puts them out of reach for an independent business at this scale. Inventory management platforms at the small business price point handle reorder thresholds for shelf-stable goods, not perishable item ordering where the decision window is "how many of this do I need for the next three days."

None of the tools I found solve the item-level ordering decision use case for a small independent business at a price point that makes sense. That gap — item-level ordering intelligence, external factor integration, small business pricing — is the specific problem this tool addresses. It's also why the same problem almost certainly exists at every independent business running a modern POS system that captures this level of transaction data.

The technical architecture

The stack was chosen to be deployable, maintainable, and extensible — specifically designed so that a future ML layer could be added without rebuilding the foundation.

Frontend React — staff-facing ordering interface, accessible from any phone or browser. Inventory input screen for current shelf counts, recommended order quantities per item as output.
Backend FastAPI (Python) — handles data pipeline, statistical calculations, and external API integration. Python chosen specifically for future ML extensibility.
Database PostgreSQL — stores historical sales records, with schema designed to accept retroactive weather and school calendar attachment. Historical context columns are NULL now, populated as data becomes available.
Deployment Vercel + Render — frontend on Vercel, backend on Render. Both have free tiers appropriate for this scale.
External API Open-Meteo — free weather API, no key required, supports both historical data and forecasts. Historical weather data retroactively attached to existing sales records to build a training dataset for Phase 2.
Phase 1 logic Statistical averaging by day of week, applied to the specific coverage window of each order day. Deterministic, auditable, explainable — no black box.
Phase 2 path Historical weather + school calendar data retroactively attached to existing records. ML layer added on top of the same data structure without schema changes.

The intentional design decision here: Phase 1 produces useful recommendations immediately using statistical averaging. Phase 2 makes those recommendations more accurate using the historical context that Phase 1 is quietly building in the background. The architecture supports the future without requiring it.

The proposal

The deliverable at this stage was a formal contractor proposal — not a rough estimate, but a scoped document covering the business problem, the gap analysis, the competitive landscape, the phased technical plan, and the pricing with justification built from actual sales figures.

The pricing was positioned deliberately below market rate — roughly a third of what a comparable custom build would cost from an independent development shop. That gap was justified in the proposal using the business's own revenue data: given average daily sales volume and the ordering decision window, the tool pays for itself in reduced waste within a predictable timeframe. That's not a claim made in the abstract — it's grounded in the numbers that were already in the analysis.

The proposal also included a pre-build checklist: specific data cleanup and categorization steps that needed to happen in the POS before the build could begin, so the data pipeline would have clean inputs. Catching that before work started was the difference between a smooth Phase 1 and a Phase 1 that gets blocked by data quality problems halfway through.

Why scoping before building matters

Every hour spent on the wrong architecture or the wrong scope costs more to undo than it cost to build. A proposal that defines the problem clearly, identifies the constraints, specifies what's in and out of scope, and surfaces the pre-build dependencies isn't overhead — it's the work that makes the build go correctly.

In this case the proposal also functions as a standalone artifact: if the project doesn't move forward, the analysis and scoping document still exist as a complete record of what was found and why. That's intentional. Good scoping work should be valuable regardless of what happens next.

The larger pattern

This is the third time I've identified a version of the same problem at the same business: the data exists, the decision is being made without it, and the gap is the absence of a tool or framework that makes the data usable at the moment it's needed. Staffing, ordering, operational documentation — all three had the same root cause.

That pattern isn't specific to this business. It exists at every small independent business running a modern POS system that captures transaction data it never fully uses. The data infrastructure is already there. The intelligence layer on top of it isn't.

Building that layer — identifying the gap, analyzing what's available, designing something that fits the actual workflow, and delivering it in a form the business can use and maintain — is the work I find most interesting and the work I'm most equipped to do. The sandwich ordering tool is one instance of it. It won't be the last.
