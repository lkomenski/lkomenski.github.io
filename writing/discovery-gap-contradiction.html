When your KPI and your chart contradict each other — and both are coming from the same data

SQL Server · Stored Procedures · Data QA · 12 min read · Hidden Gem Music Capstone

From the project

Hidden Gem Music

Cross-regional music discovery platform · 28M rows · SQL Server · .NET 9

View Project →

The Global Overlap Dashboard had two pieces of information about the Discovery Gap — how long it takes a song to spread from its origin country to another market. The KPI card showed an average of 43 days. The histogram directly below it showed the 0–7 day bucket as by far the tallest bar, with the distribution dropping sharply after the first week.

A 43-day average is not consistent with a distribution that heavy in the first week. Both numbers came from the same underlying data. One of them was wrong — or both were measuring something different than I thought.

This is the investigation that followed: what I ran, what I found, and what I changed. There were three separate root causes, and fixing the first one revealed the second, which revealed the third. None of them were obvious until I started pulling the data directly.

How I noticed it

I was doing a full comb-through of the dashboard after wiring it to live data — checking that every number felt right, not just that it rendered. The contradiction between the KPI card and the histogram jumped out visually. A 43-day average should produce a histogram that leans right, not one that peaks in the first week and drops sharply. Something was off.

The first thing I did was run the stored procedures directly against the database to see what they were actually returning. 

EXEC sp_GetAverageDiscoveryGap @DateStart = '2017-01-01', @DateEnd = '2025-06-10';
EXEC sp_GetDiscoveryGapDistribution @DateStart = '2017-01-01', @DateEnd = '2025-06-10';

The outputs confirmed what I was seeing on the dashboard. Then I looked at the stored procedure code for both.

Root cause 1: The two procedures were measuring different things

sp_GetAverageDiscoveryGap was averaging days_to_spread across every row in DiscoveryGapByDay. That table has one row per song-country pair — so a song that spread to ten countries contributed ten rows to the average, one per destination country. A song that crossed quickly to nine countries but slowly to one pulled the average up by that one slow country, weighted equally with the fast ones.

sp_GetDiscoveryGapDistribution used COUNT(DISTINCT song_id) per bucket — each song counted once, placed in the bucket of its minimum gap across all its destination countries.

These are fundamentally different aggregation units. The average was counting destination-country crossings. The histogram was counting songs. They were never going to agree.

The mismatch

Average SP: one row per song-country crossing, averaged across all crossings.
Distribution SP: one row per song, placed by minimum crossing time.
Same table. Same column. Completely different denominators.

The fix was to rewrite sp_GetAverageDiscoveryGap to aggregate to one row per song first — taking MIN(days_to_spread) — before computing the average. Both procedures now count the same unit: one first crossing per song. 

-- New SongFirstCrossing CTE in sp_GetAverageDiscoveryGap
WITH SongFirstCrossing AS (
    SELECT
        song_id,
        MIN(days_to_spread) AS days_to_spread
    FROM DiscoveryGapByDay
    WHERE days_to_spread > 0
    GROUP BY song_id
)

After the fix, the average dropped from 43 days to 7 days. The median was 0. Which was a different problem.

Root cause 2: 55,276 day-zero artifacts from dataset boundaries

A median of 0 means more than half the songs in the dataset had a measured discovery gap of zero days. That's not a real finding — it's a data artifact.

I queried the distribution directly to see what was happening: 

SELECT
    days_to_spread,
    COUNT(*) AS song_count
FROM DiscoveryGapByDay
WHERE days_to_spread = 0
GROUP BY days_to_spread;

55,276 songs had days_to_spread = 0 out of 59,922 total songs in the 0–7 day bucket. These are left-censored observations.

Dataset 2 begins on October 17, 2023. Songs that were already globally popular before that date — charting in dozens of countries simultaneously on day one of the data — produce a gap of zero because the dataset sees them appear everywhere at once. The crossing happened before data collection began. We can't measure something we don't have data for.

What left-censorship means here

Left-censored data is data where the event of interest happened before the observation window opened. We know the song spread — we just don't know when, because it was before our earliest data point. A gap of zero doesn't mean it spread instantly. It means we can't tell.

The fix: change the population stored procedure filter from gap_days >= 0 to gap_days > 0, excluding day-zero rows from the table entirely. These are not valid discovery gap measurements — they are artifacts of the dataset's start date. Repopulated the table.

After the fix: average 38 days, median 4 days. The median was now a real number. But the 0–7 day bucket was still very large — 21,936 songs. So I looked at those too.

Root cause 3: Opening-week artifacts from both dataset boundaries

Were those 21,936 fast-crossing songs real, or were they another boundary artifact? I queried to see how many had their first appearance in either dataset's opening week: 

-- How many 0-7d songs have their origin in DS2's opening week?
SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT song_id)
FROM DiscoveryGapByDay
WHERE days_to_spread BETWEEN 1 AND 7
AND song_id IN (
    SELECT song_id FROM DiscoveryGapByDay
    WHERE first_appearance BETWEEN '2023-10-17' AND '2023-10-23'
);

21,920 of the 21,936 fast-crossing songs had their origin in the DS2 opening week. Songs that appeared in the data for the first time in the opening week of a new dataset could produce artificially short gaps — the "origin" country is whichever country happened to chart them first in the data, not necessarily where they actually originated.

A HAVING clause was added to the Origin CTE in the population procedure to exclude songs whose global first appearance fell in either dataset's opening week. Repopulated again.

Why median is the right number to report — and why we show both

A mean of 108 days and a median of 26 days from the same dataset isn't a contradiction. It's information about the shape of the distribution. The long tail — songs that take 90+ days to cross — pulls the mean up significantly without affecting the median. Most songs cross in under a month. A small number take much longer and drag the average with them.

For a skewed distribution like this, the median is the more meaningful KPI. It tells you what a typical song does. The mean tells you what the dataset does overall, including the outliers. Both are true. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions.

The dashboard shows both numbers with a "why two numbers?" explanation on the KPI card flip side — because a user who sees a mean of 108 and a median of 26 on the same card deserves to understand why they're different, not just which one to trust.

The principle

When a headline number and a distribution chart contradict each other, the contradiction is the finding. Don't pick one to trust — investigate until you know why they disagree. The answer is almost always either an aggregation unit mismatch, a data artifact at a boundary, or both.

What this investigation actually was

This wasn't a bug in the traditional sense. The stored procedures were doing what they were written to do. The issue was that what they were written to do wasn't quite right — one was measuring songs, one was measuring crossings, and neither the code nor the documentation made that distinction visible until the numbers stopped agreeing with each other on the dashboard.

The dashboard is what caught it. Having both the KPI and the distribution visible on the same screen meant the inconsistency was immediately obvious to anyone paying attention. If either of those had been on a different page — or if I'd only verified the numbers in isolation — this would have shipped wrong.

But the deeper point isn't about the sequence of queries I ran. It's about what it means to be responsible for the data layer of a product.

When you build the data infrastructure, you are responsible for what the user sees. Not just technically — the number renders, the chart loads — but accurately. A user who looks at a KPI card and trusts that number is trusting you. If that number is wrong, or misleading, or measuring something subtly different than what the label implies, that's not a frontend problem and it's not the user's problem to figure out. It's yours.

That responsibility has two parts. The first is knowing what you're looking at well enough to notice when something feels off — which requires actually looking, not just verifying that the queries run. The second is knowing how to get to the root cause technically: which layer to check, which query to run, what the output means, and how to fix it cleanly without introducing a new problem.

Showing both mean and median on the KPI card wasn't just a design choice. It was an acknowledgment that a single number would have been misleading — and that users deserve to understand the shape of the data they're looking at, not just the headline. That decision came from the same place as the investigation: if you're responsible for the data, you're responsible for representing it honestly.

The investigation sequence — visual check, direct SP execution, code review, direct table query, boundary analysis, fix, repopulate, verify — is reproducible and it works. But the thing that starts it is simpler: you have to actually look at what you built and ask whether it's right.
