Why Viral 50 exclusion has to happen at the population layer, not the read layer

SQL Server · Stored Procedures · Data Integrity · 10 min read · Hidden Gem Music Capstone

From the project

Hidden Gem Music

Cross-regional music discovery platform · 28M rows · SQL Server · .NET 9

View Project →

Dataset 1 contains two chart types: the Top 200 and the Viral 50. They are both stored in FACT_ChartEntry, distinguished by chart_type_id. The Viral 50 entries are labeled clearly in the data. For a long time, that felt like enough.

It wasn't. The label doesn't protect you from what happens when you mix two fundamentally different measurements into the same calculation. Every summary table in the database was contaminated by Viral 50 entries — and because all read stored procedures query pre-computed summary tables, the fix had to happen at the population layer, not the read layer. This post is about how I caught it, why the fix location matters, and what the KPI values looked like before and after.

What the Viral 50 actually measures

The Top 200 ranks songs by raw stream count — sustained listener demand. A song on the Top 200 is there because people are actively choosing to listen to it.

The Viral 50 ranks songs by rate-of-spread — shares, playlist adds, social media activity, growing buzz. A song on the Viral 50 is gaining momentum. It may have very few total streams. It is not necessarily a song people are choosing to listen to in large numbers yet.

The core distinction

Top 200: sustained demand. People are listening to this.
Viral 50: rate of spread. People are sharing this.

These are different signals. They should never be averaged together, and they should never contribute to the same overlap calculation.

The Hidden Gem Music project is built around a specific question: which songs are genuinely popular across multiple countries that haven't crossed over into a given local market? "Genuinely popular" means sustained listener demand. The Viral 50 doesn't measure that. Including it in the overlap calculations meant the answer to our core question was partially wrong.

How I caught it

I was doing a full comb-through of the dashboard after the Discovery Gap investigation was resolved. I was looking at the Global Overlap charts — checking that the numbers felt right, that the trends made sense, that the picture the dashboard was painting was analytically defensible.

Something felt off. The charts were labeled correctly — they said what they were showing. But I started asking a different question: not "is this labeled right?" but "is what this is showing actually meaningful?"

The Viral 50 doesn't appear anywhere else in the application. The Hidden Gems logic, the country comparisons, the globe summaries — all of those are built around the concept of sustained chart presence, which means Top 200 and Top 50 data. The Viral 50 had never been part of the analytical story we were telling. It was only in the summary tables because it was in FACT_ChartEntry and nobody had explicitly excluded it from the population procedures.

Once I saw that, the question became: what is a user actually concluding when they look at a "26% global overlap rate"? Are they concluding that 26% of charting songs appear in multiple countries by sustained listener demand? Or are they concluding that 26% of songs appear in multiple countries by some mix of demand and viral spread that we've never defined or disclosed?

The second one is not a meaningful number. And putting it on a dashboard as though it is would be misleading.

Why the fix had to happen at the population layer

The database architecture uses pre-computed summary tables for all read operations. The 28 million rows in FACT_ChartEntry are never queried at request time — every API call reads from a summary table that was pre-populated by a population stored procedure. This is what makes the application fast.

It also means that if the contamination is in the summary tables, it can't be fixed in the read layer. You can't add a WHERE chart_type_id != 2 filter to a read SP that's querying SongCountryPresence — the Viral 50 data is already baked into every row of that table. The fix has to happen where the summary tables are built.

Three population stored procedures were the root cause — the ones that touch FACT_ChartEntry directly: 

-- Added to all three population SPs:
AND ce.chart_type_id != 2  -- Exclude Viral 50

The three procedures that needed the fix: 

sp_PopulateSongCountryPresence   -- main WHERE clause
sp_PopulateDiscoveryGapByDay     -- FirstAppearance CTE
sp_PopulatePeakReachBySong       -- DailyReach CTE

The other population procedures don't touch FACT_ChartEntry directly — they build from the already-fixed summary tables. So fixing those three was sufficient.

After applying the fix, all six summary tables had to be repopulated in strict dependency order, because each table feeds into the next: 

sp_PopulateSongCountryPresence
  → sp_PopulateDiscoveryGapByDay
    → sp_PopulatePeakReachBySong
      → sp_PopulateGlobalOverlapByYear
        → sp_PopulateCountryYearStats
          → sp_PopulateIsolationScoreByCountry

During repopulation, a regression surfaced: the gap_days > 0 filter in sp_PopulateDiscoveryGapByDay had reverted to >= 0 during the SP rewrite — re-introducing the day-zero artifact problem that had been fixed in the previous investigation. Caught it during verification, corrected it, repopulated again.

What changed in the numbers

Metric Pre-fix Post-fix
Global Overlap Rate 26% 25%
Discovery Gap median 4 days 12 days
Discovery Gap mean 38 days 108 days
Peak Cross-Regional Reach 70 countries — abcdefu by GAYLE 69 countries — STAY by The Kid LAROI & Justin Bieber

The shifts are meaningful. A discovery gap median of 4 days versus 12 days tells a different story about how music spreads. The Viral 50 inflated the apparent speed of crossover — songs going viral spread faster than songs gaining sustained popularity, so including them pulled all the gap metrics down.

The peak reach winner also changed — which matters for any user who would have looked at that KPI and concluded that abcdefu was the most globally reaching song in the dataset. It wasn't, by the metric we actually care about. STAY was.

The label isn't enough

The Viral 50 entries were always labeled correctly. chart_type_id = 2 was in the data from day one. The charts on the dashboard said what they were showing. None of that was the problem.

The problem was that a correct label on mixed data still produces a misleading result if the mixing itself is wrong. A user looking at a "global overlap rate" doesn't see the chart type breakdown — they see a percentage that's supposed to tell them something about how music crosses borders. If that percentage is contaminated by a metric that measures something different, the label doesn't save them.

The question I started asking — not "is this labeled right?" but "is what this is showing actually meaningful?" — is a different question. It requires understanding what the data actually measures, not just what it's called. And it requires being willing to slow down on something that looks fine on the surface and ask whether it actually is.

The fix was three lines of SQL across three stored procedures and a repopulation run. The investigation that got there took considerably longer — and was worth every minute of it, because the numbers that shipped were the right ones.
