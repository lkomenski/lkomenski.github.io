Operations · Analytics · Workforce Planning

Using POS transaction data to fix a staffing problem nobody could articulate

Operational Analytics · Process Design · 10 min read

From the role

Assistant Manager & Operational Systems Lead

Local food & beverage business · Tacoma, WA · 2023–Present

Four people left within days of each other at the end of May — including the manager who had been handling scheduling for years. The incoming manager had never worked a shift on our floor. There was no documented staffing framework to hand off, no data-backed baseline to work from, and June — the café's busiest month — was days away.

The deeper problem wasn't the staffing crisis itself. It was that the previous scheduling approach had never been connected to sales data. Schedules were built from habit, availability, and feel. Nobody knew whether staffing levels were actually matched to when the business needed people. The crisis made that visible because there was nothing to fall back on except institutional knowledge that was walking out the door.

Nobody asked me to build a framework. I proposed treating the scheduling rebuild as a contractor project and used the transition as the opportunity to create what should have existed already.

What I pulled and why the segmentation mattered

All data came from the café's POS system. The obvious approach — export everything at once and look at the aggregate — is the wrong approach for staffing decisions. Aggregate data tells you total volume. It doesn't tell you that Saturday spikes immediately at opening while Tuesday ramps gradually, or that slow season looks nothing like June. Averaging across all days and all seasons flattens exactly the variation staffing decisions depend on.

The pull was structured in three layers:

14 months of daily labor data — actual hours worked, wages paid, sales and transactions per day. This established the per-person throughput figure: $111 per hour per person at peak. That number became the foundation for every staffing threshold in the model — if an hour is generating enough revenue to justify a second person, $111 is when you add them.

Monthly hourly sales files — sales and transaction counts broken down by hour across all days of the week, covering 14 months. This gave the revenue curve shape across the full period.

21 day-of-week specific hourly files — the most important pull. The POS hourly report filtered by individual day of week across three distinct seasons: summer 2025, slow season (December–March), and spring 2026. This separated Friday's revenue curve from Tuesday's, and made Saturday's immediate morning spike visible as structurally distinct from Sunday's. Without this level of segmentation, those day-specific patterns disappear.

Two data quality issues had to be corrected before the model was usable. The store expanded its opening hours in November 2025, which meant pre-November data showed $0 for early morning hours that are now open — a confound that would have made morning staffing look unjustified when it's actually among the most justified. The June projection also required a weighted blend: summer data weighted at 60%, spring data at 40%, because June sits between the two seasons and neither alone would have been representative.

What the data showed

Finding 01

A consistent 2-hour solo window every morning — on every day of the week

The previous schedule had mid shifts starting at 10 or 10:30am across the board, regardless of day. The data showed the $200+/hr threshold — the point where two people are justified — being crossed at 8am on every single day of the week. That's a two-hour window every morning where one person was handling volume that warranted two. It wasn't visible as a staffing problem because it had always been that way. The data made it undeniable.

Finding 02

Weekends aren't busier weekdays — they're structurally different days

Saturday and Sunday both jump to three to four people needed almost immediately at opening. There's no gradual weekday ramp. The previous schedule had treated weekends as slightly busier weekdays — same shape, more volume. The revenue curves showed they're not the same shape. Weekend staffing needs to be front-loaded from the start of the day, not scaled from a weekday template.

Finding 03

Thursday's floor feel was backed up by the numbers

Thursday had always felt genuinely busy in a way that was hard to explain — not peak volume, but consistently demanding. The data confirmed it. That justified keeping three mid slots on Thursdays rather than trimming them as I'd initially considered. This is the kind of decision that only makes sense if you trust both the data and the floor experience, because the data alone doesn't tell you why Thursday feels the way it does — only that the volume supports what you're feeling.

Finding 04

Wednesday's complexity doesn't show in the sales curve

Wednesday is a heavy delivery coordination day. The back-of-house load is real and consistent — but it doesn't produce the kind of transaction volume that shows up as a spike in hourly sales data. If the model had been applied mechanically, Wednesday would have looked like a lighter staffing day than it actually is. It had to be treated as a floor constraint rather than a data-driven one, because the data undersells how hard Wednesday actually is to work. That call required the floor context to make correctly.

What changed

The output was a multi-tab Excel workbook: two schedule grids (one for the immediate period, one accounting for a staff member returning from leave), an hourly sales flow tab showing the full revenue curve by day and season, a shift framework with data justification for each slot, and a roster with accurate hours and wages per person.

The practical changes: staggered arrivals that match the revenue curve rather than a flat 10am start across the board. A consistent rule that no barista clocks on after noon. A hard two-person bar close every day. Shift end times set by when coverage can be maintained, not by arbitrary fixed lengths.

The labor analysis also answered the hiring question directly. The operation was running at 11–13.5% labor cost against a 20% industry cap — meaning there was genuine room to bring someone on and promise them meaningful hours, not one or two shifts cobbled together from gaps. That's a different conversation than "we think we need more people." It's a defensible answer with numbers behind it.

What the model couldn't do alone

The analytical work and the operational reality were happening in parallel. Constraints kept shifting — people were hired, people left, availability changed. Several staffing decisions required context that doesn't appear anywhere in a POS export: which staff can and can't work alone with whom, which days have coordination complexity that doesn't show in sales volume, whose availability rules need to be built around regardless of what the curve says.

A correct model that ignores those constraints produces a schedule that works on paper and breaks on the first day. The floor experience is what lets you know which constraints are real and which ones can be pushed back on. The analysis took over a week of refinement — not because the data was difficult, but because getting it right meant iterating against a reality that kept moving.

The gap this filled

Most small businesses run on institutional knowledge that lives in people, not in systems. When those people leave, the knowledge goes with them. A new manager stepping into a scheduling role with no documented baseline is not a special situation. It's the default.

What made this solvable was the combination of knowing how to get the data, knowing what to do with it analytically, and having enough firsthand operational context to interpret what the numbers actually meant in practice — and to catch where the model would have been wrong without that context.

I was the only person in the building who could do all three. That's not a boast — it's just an accurate description of why this kind of work tends not to get done in small businesses, and what it takes to do it well.
